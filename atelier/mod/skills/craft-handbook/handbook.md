# The Craft Handbook — the common core

*What makes a novel hold us, and how it is made, whatever the genre.*

---

## Before reading

**What this handbook is.** A workshop manual: the principles, tools and debates of the
novelist's craft. It is not a *bible*, a word the trade keeps for the bible of one work (the
world, characters, chronology and rules of a given novel or series). Each novel has its own.
The handbook serves them all.

**How it is written.** Each principle is tied to its author. Whatever has no named source is
a synthesis, and is marked **[synthesis]**. This matters: most "rules of writing" are
positions held by a school, not laws. When one school contradicts another, the disagreement
is shown under the mark ⚖️ instead of being settled in silence.

**Two uses.** A writer browses it. Claude uses it when advising on a plot or a page, citing
the principle and its author rather than "memory". Volumes for particular genres may follow.

# Part I — Why a novel holds us

## 1. The vivid and continuous dream

John Gardner (*The Art of Fiction*, 1983) gives the best definition of being "caught up":
successful fiction creates in the reader's mind a **vivid and continuous dream**.

- **Vivid**: the reader knows who is there, where, what they want and why. If the reader
  hesitates, their emotions blur.
- **Continuous**: nothing wakes the reader. Gardner counts it among the major faults to let
  the reader's mind leave the dream, even for a moment.

**What wakes the reader, according to Gardner:** a word out of register, an overloaded
sentence, an accidental rhyme, an abrupt jump in narrative distance (see § 12), and what he
calls faults of soul: sentimentality, frigidity, mannerism.

> **What to take from it.** Every other rule in this handbook serves this dream or
> interrupts it. Facing a page that "doesn't work", the first question is: *at which word
> did the reader wake up?* [synthesis]

## 2. The question that pulls the reader

A reader turns the page because a question is open and they want the answer.
**[synthesis]** Schools name this question differently:
- the *dramatic question*: will the protagonist reach their goal? (Lavandier, *La
  Dramaturgie*, 1994; in English, *Writing Drama*);
- the *promises* made to the reader, which must be kept (Sanderson, see § 7);
- *curiosity*, or the *inquiry* of the MICE model (§ 10).

There is always a big one, the book's, and many small ones: the scene's, the paragraph's,
the line's.

### Suspense versus surprise

The most useful example in the trade comes from Hitchcock, in his interviews with Truffaut
(*Hitchcock/Truffaut*, 1966). Two people talk at a table. Under the table, a bomb.
- If the audience does not know and the bomb goes off: **surprise**, fifteen seconds of
  effect.
- If the audience saw the bomb planted and can see the clock: the same dull conversation
  becomes unbearable. **Suspense**, fifteen minutes of effect.

His conclusion: **whenever possible, the audience should be informed.** He added elsewhere
that the bomb need not go off.

### Dramatic irony

This is the mechanism the bomb runs on: **the reader knows what a character does not.**
Lavandier sees it as the one great mechanism that does not derive from the goal/obstacle
pair. It also works the other way round: a character knows what the reader does not, and the
gap drives the reading.

> **What to take from it.** Before writing a revelation, ask what would be gained by giving
> it to the reader *earlier*, and hiding it only from the character. [synthesis]

## 3. Desire, obstacle, conflict

The heart of classical dramaturgy fits in one chain: **a character wants something and meets
obstacles; conflict arises, and conflict produces emotion** (Lavandier). Several
consequences:

- **The goal must be hard**, or nobody cares. But not impossible: dosing the obstacles is,
  for Lavandier, one of the hardest parts of the craft.
- **A passive protagonist makes a passive reader.** An intense desire is needed.
- **There are two kinds of obstacle.** *External* obstacles (the world, the adversary) and
  *internal* ones (the character's flaws). Melodrama piles up the first; tragedy rests on the
  second: the tragic flaw *is* an internal obstacle.
- The strongest conflict is not the most physical. It is the one the character **creates
  for themselves** through their flaws.

Lavandier sums up the recipe for a good story in three words: conflict, conflict and
conflict.

⚖️ **The counterpoint: Ursula K. Le Guin** (*Steering the Craft*, 1998, revised 2015). She
criticises modern manuals for equating story with conflict, which in her view reflects a
culture that overvalues aggression and competition. Conflict is only one behaviour among
others: relating, finding, losing, bearing, discovering, parting, changing. **For her, story
is change.** Her essay *The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction* extends the idea.

> **What to take from it.** The two positions reconcile better than they seem to. What holds
> the reader is **a change that costs something**. Conflict is the most common way to make it
> cost, not the only one: a loss, a discovery or a parting cost too. [synthesis]

## 4. Inner change: what the story is really about

Lisa Cron (*Wired for Story*, 2012; *Story Genius*, 2016) goes further: **the plot is not the
story.** The plot exists to force the protagonist into a necessary inner change, that is, to
give up a **misbelief** about what makes people act. That belief formed long before page 1,
often in childhood, and it produces both the character's behaviour and the plot.

The same idea appears under other names:
- **Edgerton** (*Hooked*, 2007) separates the *surface problem*, external and immediate, which
  drives the first scenes, from the *story-worthy problem*, the inner problem deep enough to
  fill a whole book. Solving the first must not solve the second.
- **Truby** (*The Anatomy of Story*, 2007) sets *desire* (what the character pursues) against
  *need* (what they must correct in themselves), and leads the story to a
  *self-revelation*.
- **The MICE model** (§ 10) calls this a *Character* thread: it opens on "who am I?" and
  closes when the character recognises themselves.

> **What to take from it.** For each protagonist, four lines: what they **want**, what they
> **need**, the **misbelief** that separates them from it, and the **past episode** that
> installed it. If these four lines do not exist, introspective scenes go round in circles:
> the character thinks, but nothing changes in how they think. [synthesis, after Cron and
> Truby]

## 5. Tension on every page

Donald Maass (*Writing the Breakout Novel*, 2001; *The Fire in Fiction*, 2009) makes it a rule
of the line. Every paragraph, ideally every line, should plant in the reader a small worry, a
question or an apprehension, which the reader will unconsciously try to settle by reading on.
He calls this **micro-tension**.

Micro-tension does not come from big events. It comes from **the character's conflicting
emotions, and the friction between characters**, even in an ordinary conversation. Maass
observes that tensionless dialogue in a manuscript is the first place a professional reader
starts to skim.

> **The test.** Take a page at random. Where is the open question in this paragraph? If there
> is none, that is where the reader skips lines. [synthesis, after Maass]

## 6. Attaching to a character

Why follow someone for four hundred pages? In his lectures at Brigham Young University (on
YouTube, see the bibliography), Brandon Sanderson proposes **three sliders**:
- **sympathy**: we like them, or understand their reasons;
- **competence**: they are good at something;
- **proactivity**: they act instead of enduring.

A character does not need all three at maximum: one high slider compensates for a low one. An
unlikeable but very competent and very active character can carry a novel. What bores is
**all three sliders low at once**.

This meets Lavandier (§ 3), for whom a passive protagonist makes a passive reader.

> **What to take from it.** At the start of a novel, when a character is shown suffering,
> dreaming or brooding, all three sliders are often low. It is a common diagnosis of slow
> openings. [synthesis]

## 7. Promises, progress, payoff

Sanderson reduces a plot to three moves: **promise, show progress, keep the promise.**
Everything the opening hints at (tone, genre, conflicts, mysteries) is a promise. A reader
forgives a great deal as long as they feel the book is moving towards what was promised. What
they do not forgive is an ending that keeps a promise nobody made them.

The same principle exists under other names:
- **Chekhov's gun**, a rule attributed to the playwright: a gun shown in the first act must
  be used before the end. Conversely, what is used at the end must have been set up. This is
  *preparation* in Lavandier: as soon as a character states their goal, everything that
  follows depends on it.
- **Coincidences**: the 19th of Emma Coats's "22 rules" (a Pixar story artist, 2011). A
  coincidence that gets a character into trouble is a good device; one that gets them out is
  cheating.

# Part II — The mechanics

## 8. Overall structure

There are several frameworks, and they overlap more than they oppose each other.

| Framework | Author | What it brings |
|---|---|---|
| **Three acts** | Aristotle (*Poetics*), Syd Field for film, Lavandier | Exposition and inciting incident; rising obstacles; climax and answer to the dramatic question |
| **The five commandments** | Shawn Coyne (*The Story Grid*, 2015) | Inciting incident, progressive complications up to a turning point, crisis (a dilemma: the lesser evil, or two irreconcilable goods), climax (the choice in action, which reveals the character), resolution. **They apply to the book and to each scene.** |
| **The seven steps** | John Truby | Weakness and need, desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation, new equilibrium. A structure that starts from the character, not the page |
| **The hero's journey** | Joseph Campbell, then Christopher Vogler for screenwriters | Twelve stages drawn from myth. Very present in fantasy |
| **The beat sheet** | Blake Snyder (*Save the Cat!*, 2005), adapted to the novel by Jessica Brody (2018) | Fifteen beats placed at percentages of the book. The most prescriptive of all |

⚖️ **The debate.** The most precise frameworks (Snyder, Brody) are effective *diagnostic*
tools, but they become moulds if one writes *for* them: the result is interchangeable books.
Le Guin, again, points out that a story can be built without a central conflict. The
Japanese structure **kishōtenketsu** is an example: introduction, development, twist,
conclusion, and the twist is not a confrontation. **[synthesis]** Good use: write freely,
then lay the framework over the text to see where it gives way.

## 9. The scene: the unit that does the work

**A scene turns a value.** McKee (*Story*, 1997): every scene moves a value important to the
character (life/death, love/hate, trust/betrayal…) from one charge to the other. **A scene
where nothing turns is a report**, not a scene. Coyne applies his five commandments to every
scene (§ 8).

**The scene/sequel cycle** of Dwight Swain (*Techniques of the Selling Writer*, 1965) is the
most concrete pacing tool there is:
- **Scene**: *goal* (what the character wants now, in this conversation) → *conflict* (an
  opposing force, preferably active) → *disaster*. The disaster takes three forms: **no**;
  **yes, but** (they get what they wanted, with a terrible complication); **no, and
  furthermore** (they fail and it gets worse). The only outcome that kills momentum is a
  **yes** without complication.
- **Sequel**: *reaction* (emotional, immediate) → *dilemma* (every option costs) →
  *decision*, which becomes the goal of the next scene.

**The pacing diagnosis that follows** (taken up by many editors, including September C.
Fawkes):
- a "breathless" book: sequels too short or missing;
- a book that "drags": sequels too long;
- a book "without direction": sequels that do not end in a decision.

⚖️ Fawkes points out that the cycle is not applied mechanically. Elements can be skipped,
reversed or layered.

## 10. Several threads, and the order they close: MICE

The **MICE** model (Orson Scott Card, as MACE, then taken up and developed by Mary Robinette
Kowal in the podcast *Writing Excuses*, season 16) distinguishes four kinds of thread. Each
opens and closes in its own way:

| Thread | Opens when… | Closes when… | Its conflicts… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Milieu** (the place) | we enter a place | we leave it | prevent leaving |
| **Inquiry** (the question) | a question is raised | it is answered | prevent answering |
| **Character** | "who am I?" | they recognise themselves | block the change |
| **Event** | order is broken | an order, old or new, is restored | prevent the return to order |

**The nesting rule**: threads close in the reverse order of their opening, like brackets
(*first opened, last closed*). An ending that "falls flat" often closes threads in the wrong
order, or leaves open the one the opening promised.

> **What to take from it.** This is the most useful tool for a **series**: each book closes
> its inner threads and leaves at least one outer thread open. [synthesis, after Kowal]

## 11. Pacing

Pacing is not speed. **It is alternation.** **[synthesis]** One alternates:
- scene and sequel (§ 9);
- action and breathing space;
- scene (time told live) and summary (time compressed);
- short sentences (in shock) and long ones (in contemplation).

A leaden pace nearly always comes from **repetition**: the same emotion revisited without
progress, the same information given again. The reader is not bored because it is slow; they
are bored because they already know. **[synthesis]**

## 12. Point of view and narrative distance

**Point of view** is the choice of who perceives: first person, close third person, omniscient,
or several points of view in turn. Each opens some things and closes others. Dramatic irony,
for instance, is easier with several points of view; intimacy with one.

**Narrative distance** (*psychic distance*, Gardner) measures the gap between the narration and
the character's mind. Gardner illustrates it with five sentences that move closer step by
step:
1. a historical frame: a date, a man seen from afar;
2. his full name, and a measured comment on what he feels;
3. his first name, and a feeling stated simply;
4. **free indirect speech**: the narration takes on the character's voice, his oaths, his
   vocabulary;
5. fusion: raw sensation, no narrator.

**The rule**: one can move between these levels, but **gradually and on purpose**. A jump
from level 1 to level 5 for no reason wakes the reader (§ 1).

> **What to take from it.** Introspection works at level 4 or 5, where the reader thinks
> *with* the character. Told at level 2 or 3, it becomes a report on what the character
> thinks, and the reader drifts off. [synthesis]

## 13. Beginnings and endings

**The beginning.** Les Edgerton (*Hooked*) holds a clear position: start where the trouble
starts, that is at the **inciting incident**, and feed in context and backstory later. The
opening scene should:
- set a surface problem;
- hint at the deep problem without revealing it;
- leave the protagonist **worse off** than at the start of the scene.

He advises against opening on a dream, on waking up, or on dialogue.

⚖️ Some readers of his book point out that many great novels start before the inciting
incident, and that he repeats the rule to the point of dogma. A slow opening can hold **if it
carries a question** (§ 2) and a character with one high slider (§ 6). **[synthesis]**

**The ending.** It keeps the promises (§ 7), closes the threads in the right order (§ 10), and
shows the change (§ 4) **through an act, not a statement**: the climax is a choice put into
action (Coyne). The inciting incident must be resolved: a story, Coyne reminds us, tells how an
unexpected change is digested. **[synthesis, after Coyne and Sanderson]**

## 14. Dialogue and exposition

- **A dialogue is a miniature scene**: each person wants something from the other. Without
  that, it is idle talk without tension (Maass, § 5).
- **Subtext**: what matters is what the characters do not say, or say sideways.
  **[synthesis]**
- **Activity** (Lavandier): give the characters something to do while they talk. The gesture
  can contradict the words.
- **Exposition**: give information when the reader needs it, not before. The classic fault is
  "*As you know, Bob*", where one character explains to another what both already know. It
  appears in many manuals and on TV Tropes. The remedy: pass the information **through a
  conflict**. Someone does not know it, opposes it, or pays dearly for it. **[synthesis]**

# Part III — Principles: good, bad, and double-edged

| Principle | What it protects | When it becomes harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **"Show, don't tell"** | The continuous dream (§ 1): the reader lives instead of being informed | When everything must be shown: a well-placed summary gains pace. What is shown is what costs emotionally; the rest can be told [synthesis] |
| **"Kill your darlings"** (attributed to Arthur Quiller-Couch, taken up by Faulkner and King) | The book comes before the fine sentence that does not serve it | When it cuts what makes the author's voice. Cut what does not serve, not what you love [synthesis] |
| **"Write what you know"** | Emotional truth | Taken literally (your jobs, your town). It is the truth of the emotion one must know, not the setting [synthesis] |
| **Few adverbs** (Stephen King, *On Writing*, 2000) | The right verb rather than a weak verb plus an adverb | Applied by counting. King mainly targets adverbs that double a speech verb ("he said sharply") |
| **Second draft = first draft minus 10%** (King, same book) | Concision through revision | For a first draft that is too thin, which happens to writers who work as architects |
| **Start *in medias res*** | The hook (§ 13) | If the reader does not yet know why they should care: action without stakes is noise |
| **The character must be likeable** | Attachment (§ 6) | False in the absolute. Competence or proactivity can compensate. What is true: the reader must *understand* the character |

**The faults that always come back** [synthesis, unless stated]:
- **a passive protagonist** who endures without desire (Lavandier);
- **stakes set too late**: the reader does not know why to worry (Edgerton);
- **repetitive introspection**: the same emotion revisited without the belief moving (§ 4,
  § 11);
- **the info-dump**: exposition without conflict (§ 14);
- **the *deus ex machina***: a problem solved by something the reader did not know about. It
  is the very subject of Sanderson's first law of magic (see the bibliography) and of Coats's
  rule on coincidences (§ 7);
- **opening on waking up**, a dream, or a character describing themselves in a mirror
  (Edgerton, and most editors);
- **an abrupt jump in narrative distance** (Gardner, § 12);
- **a scene that turns no value** (McKee, § 9);
- **an ending that closes the threads out of order** (§ 10).

# Part IV — The work: before, during, after

## 15. Architect or gardener

George R. R. Martin contrasts two temperaments. **The architect** draws up the plans before
driving the first nail. **The gardener** plants a seed and watches what grows, while still
knowing what kind of seed it was. Martin calls himself more of a gardener, and adds that nobody
is wholly one or the other. The terms *plotters* and *pantsers* are also used.

⚖️ Neither method is better. **They simply do not miss the same things.** The architect
sometimes misses life: characters carrying out a plan. The gardener often misses structure:
sagging middles and endings that do not keep the promises. The gardener repairs in revision,
the architect in the plan. **[synthesis]**

## 16. The work upstream

From lightest to heaviest:
1. **The one-sentence premise** (*logline*): *who* wants *what*, *against whom or what*, and
   *what is at stake*. If it does not fit in one sentence, the book is not yet thought
   through. **[synthesis]**
2. **The protagonist's four lines**: what they want, what they need, their misbelief, the
   episode that installed it (§ 4).
3. **The Snowflake method** (Randy Ingermanson): one sentence, then a paragraph, then a page,
   then character sheets, then a scene list. One develops by successive expansions.
4. **Lisa Cron's method** (*Story Genius*): first write the *past* scenes that built the
   misbelief, before any plot outline.
5. **The beat sheet or the five commandments** laid over the whole book (§ 8).
6. **The novel's bible**: the world, the rules, the chronology (see Part V).

## 17. While writing

- **Write the first draft with the door closed, rewrite with the door open** (King): the
  first draft is for yourself, the rewrite for the reader.
- **Do not rewrite chapter 1 while writing chapter 10.** The classic trap of first novels: an
  opening polished ten times and a book with no ending. **[synthesis]**
- **Keep a logbook**: what was decided along the way (a rule of the world, an age, a date)
  and must be carried into the bible. **[synthesis]**
- **Note promises when you make them** (§ 7): every gun laid on the table goes on a list.
  **[synthesis]**

## 18. After: revision

**[synthesis, from all the sources]**
- **Revise in passes, one concern at a time**: structure first (are these the right
  scenes?), then the scenes (does each turn a value?), then the characters, then the line
  (micro-tension, language). Polishing sentences you will later delete is wasted time.
- **Make the scene table**: one row per scene, with point of view, the value that turns, the
  goal and the disaster. The gaps show at once (the *Story Grid* method).
- **Read aloud**: the ear catches what the eye lets through.
- **Use beta readers**, and ask them *where they lost interest*, not *what they thought*.
  Where they let go is reliable. Their remedies rarely are.

# Part V — Working documents

## Necessary

| Document | What it holds | Why it is necessary |
|---|---|---|
| **The premise** | One sentence (§ 16) | It is the compass for cutting decisions |
| **The protagonist sheet** | Desire, need, misbelief, origin episode (§ 4) | Without it, introspection goes round in circles |
| **The novel's bible** | Characters (ages, ties, voices), places, chronology, rules of the world | Consistency. One wrong date and the reader wakes up (§ 1) |
| **The scene list**, even a rough one | One line per scene | The book at a glance, and it can be reorganised without rewriting |

## Useful

- **The detailed scene table** (Story Grid): point of view, value, goal, disaster, word count.
- **The MICE thread map** (§ 10): what opens, where, and in what order it closes.
- **The promise list** (§ 7): each promise made to the reader, and the chapter where it is
  kept.
- **The beat sheet** (§ 8), as a diagnostic tool.
- **The logbook** (§ 17).
- **The glossary**: proper names, invented terms, their spelling.
- **The voice sheet** for each point-of-view character: their verbal tics, their forbidden
  words, what they notice first in a room.

A character sheet template comes with this handbook: [`character-sheet.md`](character-sheet.md).

# Part VI — From novel to series

**[synthesis]**
- **Each book stands alone**: it has its question, its closed threads, its climax. It leaves
  open at least one larger thread that runs through the series (§ 10).
- **Two arcs at once**: the character's change within the book, and their change across the
  series. The second must not stop the first from landing.
- **The bible becomes essential**, and it grows: what is reserved for later books must be
  written down in it, or it will be contradicted by oversight.
- **Escalation**: each book raises the stakes, or deepens them. Raising them endlessly leads
  to one-upmanship. Deepening them means making the price more personal.
- **The reminder**: the reader of book 2 has forgotten things. Give the information again
  through action, without a summary (§ 14).
