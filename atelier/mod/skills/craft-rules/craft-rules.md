# Craft rules — M1 to M10

Rules for narrative prose, each credited to a writer or a practitioner. Each has a short id
(M1, M2…) that the `craft-rules` skill puts in the `rule` field of its margin notes.

**These rules never override the author's own style guide.** Where the style guide says
otherwise, the style guide is right, and the case is not flagged.

**On attribution.** Each rule is credited to an author as the secondary source that
presented it does (short social-media posts and videos, listed under [Sources](#sources)).
These attributions have not been checked against the primary sources (letters, essays,
memoirs). The wording of the rules is original to this file. The before/after examples come
from the secondary sources, translated, and each set is credited under its examples; M7 and
M8 have none in their source and get none here.

---

## M1 — No adjective that tells the reader what to feel · C. S. Lewis

**Credited source:** a 1956 letter to a young American reader, one of five pieces of advice
on writing.

**The rule.** Do not use an adjective whose only job is to tell the reader how to react:
*terrible, wonderful, horrifying, delightful, hideous, exquisite*, and relatives such as
*amazing, awful, incredible, beautiful*. Such a word asks the reader to do the writer's work.

**Before / after.**
- "The view from the summit was wonderful." → "From the summit she could see three
  counties and the river that divided them."
- "The cellar smelled terribly bad." → "The cellar smelled of wet dog and of something
  that had been dead for a while."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on C. S. Lewis, translated.*

**Why.** Give the reader the thing itself; they will supply the adjective.

**Limit.** The word stays **in dialogue** ("It was wonderful," she said, not looking up),
and **in a moment too small to linger on** ("They had a pleasant lunch and drove on"). Cut it
where the scene matters.

**What to check.** Every adjective of emotion or judgement in the narration: is it in a
scene that matters? Would a sensory detail do the work?

**Extension: the emotion noun.** *Credited source:* a post about Ta-Nehisi Coates's novel
*The Water Dancer*. What holds for the adjective holds for the noun: *grief, fear, longing,
tension* name a feeling so efficiently that the reader slides over them. The question to
ask: **what would the reader actually see?** Coates's narrator, who remembers everything
except his mother, never says he misses her; the novel gives him one precise image of her
dancing with a jar balanced on her head, and the missing is felt in front of it. The same
move works on an abstract idea: memory carrying people across great distances becomes a
gesture hands know, folding the land like cloth.
- "When we were growing up, there was a lot of tension in the house." → "When Dad's truck
  pulled into the yard, we turned the TV down and listened for how hard the door shut."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on Ta-Nehisi Coates, translated.*

*Limit and check:* as for the adjective.

## M2 — Nouns and verbs that do the describing · Ursula K. Le Guin

**Credited source:** an exercise she set herself as a teenager and later gave her
workshops: a descriptive paragraph with no adjectives and no adverbs.

**The rule.** Pick nouns and verbs that carry the description, instead of propping weak
ones up with adjectives and adverbs. The prose gets sharper.

**Before / after.**
- "ran quickly" → "dashed"; "a growling voice" → "a growl".
- "Emma heard a loud, sudden noise in the kitchen, got up quickly and walked silently to
  the stairs." → "Emma heard a crash in the kitchen, sprang out of bed and crept to the
  stairs."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on Ursula K. Le Guin, translated.*

**Limit.** Le Guin did not ban either; she reportedly compared them to rich desserts. "She
kissed him gently on the forehead" has no better form.

**What to check.** Every weak verb + adverb pair (*said softly, looked closely, ran fast*)
and every noun + intensity adjective: is there one verb or noun that already holds both?

*M1 and M2 are one family: the modifier asserts instead of showing. M1 targets emotion, M2
intensity and manner.*

## M3 — Write what is there, not what is missing · Ernest Hemingway

**Credited source:** the style sheet of the *Kansas City Star*, given to every young
reporter; Hemingway worked there at eighteen. The relevant rule says, in short, be positive
rather than negative.

**The rule.** Say what is there rather than what is not. The meaning is the same, but the
reader sees the character *do* something.

**Before / after.**
- did not answer → stayed silent; did not believe him → doubted him; did not look at her →
  looked away; could not sleep → lay awake.
- "The kitchen was not very clean." → "Grease coated the stove."
- "Daniel didn't like the house and didn't want to go in." → "Daniel hated the house and
  stopped at the gate."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on Ernest Hemingway, translated.*

**Why.** A reader cannot picture an absence. And a negation leaves a gap: is a meal that
was "not bad" fine, good, excellent?

**Limit.** The negation stays to **deny** ("I didn't take the money") and to **contrast**
(Shakespeare's Brutus: "Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more").
Never to dodge.

**What to check.** Every negation in the narration: does it deny, contrast, or dodge? The
three are told apart by reading, not by counting.

## M4 — The "I know" test · David Mamet

**Credited source:** a 2005 memo to the writers of the television series *The Unit*, on the
idea that an audience does not come to watch information.

**The rule.** If the other character could answer "I know", the line was written for the
reader, and the reader notices.

**Before / after.** Keep the fact, but aim the line at the other character.
- "We've been married for twelve years, Tom." → "Twelve years, Tom, and you still don't
  know how I take my coffee?"
- "You know the king banned magic after the war." → "Guards. Hide the spellbook. They've
  been hanging every mage since the war."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on David Mamet, translated.*

**What to check.** Every line that carries information: does the listener already know it?
*As you know*, *you remember that*, *like I told you* are alarms.

## M5 — The detail that stands for the rest · Stephen King

**Credited source:** *On Writing* (2000): good description is a few well-chosen details
that stand for everything else.

**The rule.** Hair and eye colour could belong to anyone. A detail that shows who the
person is gives the reader something personal to build on.

**Before / after.**
- "She was tall and thin, with long auburn hair, green eyes and a blue dress." → "She had
  her father's pointed chin and none of his patience."
- "He had brown hair, brown eyes and a scar on one eyebrow." → "He had a scar across one
  eyebrow and three versions of how he got it."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on Stephen King, translated.*

**Why.** We do not need to know exactly what they look like; we need to know how they move
through the world.

**What to check.** The **first appearance** of each main character: are all the details
physical? If so, keep one or two that say who they are.

## M6 — Dialogue as table tennis · Emily Henry

**Credited source:** a 2021 essay for *Writer's Digest*.

**The rule.** Do not let characters merely answer each other. The simplest way: pick up a
word the other has just said and send it back with a twist.

**Before / after.**
- "You're late." / "I know, sorry, there was traffic." → "You're late." / "I'm
  fashionably late." / "That would require fashion."
- "Did you finish the report?" / "Almost, I'll send it tonight." → "Did you *finish* the
  report?" / "Define 'finish'." / "Define 'employed'."

*Examples: from @senswriter's TikTok carousel on Emily Henry, translated.*

**Why.** A character who thinks fast reads as a lively mind. (The carousel also invokes
studies on dopamine: an appeal to authority the rule does not need.)

**Limit.** A technique, not a law: a whole conversation of it becomes a routine. Use it
where a polite exchange falls flat.

**What to check.** Exchanges of more than three lines where each speaker answers without
picking anything up: is there a word to send back?

## M7 — The sentence that leans forward · Lee Child

**Credited source:** remarks by Lee Child reported in a video by an editor. Child compares
good commercial fiction to a fairground ride: once on, the reader does not get off, and that
is built at the level of the sentence, not the plot.

**The rule.** Every sentence should lean forward and push the reader into the next one.
Think about what a sentence *does* as much as what it *says*: meaning is necessary, not
sufficient. The pace never drops; ideally it speeds up without anyone noticing.

**Why.** A casual reader does not feel the push; they only find they cannot stop. A book
can have the right plot and characters and still lose the reader because the sentences do
not carry them.

**Limit.** This is not "short sentences everywhere": a long sentence pushes if it moves, and
a string of short ones can stand still. Setting, chapter openings and backstory are allowed
their slowness; that is where the acceleration gathers speed.

**What to check.** In a scene that has to move: a run of sentences where nothing new
happens (a state, a habit, a comment)? A sentence that repeats the previous one? A
paragraph that stops instead of launching the next?

## M8 — An open question at the end of the chapter · Lee Child

**Credited source:** the same video. Child worked in television for years; when remote
controls arrived, viewers could leave during the break, and television answered by leaving
a question open before it. He is reported to sum up suspense elsewhere as asking a question
and delaying the answer (not verified here).

**The rule.** Raise a question (even a small or implicit one) and delay the answer. Every
chapter ending leaves something unresolved. Not necessarily an action cliffhanger: something
the reader's mind will not let go of.

**The most common fault**, according to the editor: the chapter that closes neatly. The
scene ends, the character reflects on what just happened, everything is tied up. The end of
a chapter is not the place to give the reader permission to stop.

**Why.** The reader does not decide to go on; the question decides for them. (The video
calls this "neurological", which overstates it. The nearest research is the Zeigarnik
effect, the idea that unfinished things are remembered better, whose replications are
mixed. Roland Barthes, in *S/Z*, calls the thread of riddles posed and later solved the
"hermeneutic code".)

**Limit.** One question per chapter, not a trick on every page. It does not forbid a
paragraph that ends on a flat, final sentence. A book's last chapter may resolve, though the
first book of a series gains from leaving a question for the next.

**What to check.** The last scene of each chapter, as a whole: does it end on something
unresolved, or on a closure (a departure, a return to calm, a reflection, tomorrow's plan)?
If it closes: which question in the scene could stay open one page longer?

## M9 — The first five pages: a reason to keep reading · Nikoleta Ivanova

**Credited source:** a text by Nikoleta Ivanova, a fantasy and romance ghostwriter. This is
the only rule here from a working practitioner rather than a well-known author: it stands on
its content, not on authority.

**The rule.** The first five pages do not have to explain the world, the magic system, the
politics or the hero's tragic childhood. They have to give **a reason to keep reading**,
which usually comes down to four answers:

1. **Who are we following?** A personality, a way of looking, a way of reacting. Not the
   biography: enough behaviour to make the character interesting.
2. **What is happening, now?** The immediate situation: hiding something, preparing for
   something, facing a problem. The reader does not need the whole plot, but must understand
   the scene in front of them.
3. **Why does it matter to them?** A problem becomes interesting through its consequences.
   Losing a competition leaves us cold; losing the competition that could have lifted her
   family out of poverty does not.
4. **What turns the page?** An unanswered question, an unexpected complication, an
   intriguing relationship, a conflict that promises more. No explosion or murder required:
   curiosity is enough.

*Example: from Nikoleta Ivanova's text, paraphrased.*

**Why.** On the first pages the reader has invested nothing yet; they are deciding whether
to invest. Anything that explains before it has made them want to know reads as a debt.

**Limit.** Applies only to **the opening of the book** (and, more loosely, of a part). It
does not require everything in five pages: the answers can stay partial, and the fourth
*must*. It meets M8 (the open question) at the start of the book rather than the end of a
chapter, and M5 for the protagonist's first appearance.

**What to check.** Once, on the first five pages: do the four questions have an answer, even
an implicit one? And which passages explain the world or the past before question 3 has an
answer?

## M10 — Four sentence habits · Midnight Editors

**Credited source:** a text by Midnight Editors, an editing service, listing habits cut from
almost every manuscript. None is an error; together they make the difference between
correct prose and prose that sings. Two of their habits are already covered here and are
not flagged twice: crutch adverbs (**M2**) and emotion told rather than shown (**M1**). Four
remain, each with its own label.

**M10a — The filter.** *Felt, saw, noticed, heard, realised, it seemed to her that.* These
verbs put a pane of glass between the reader and the scene: we watch the character perceive
instead of perceiving with them.
- "She heard the kettle start to whistle." → "The kettle began to whistle."
- "He noticed that the light was on." → "The light was on."

*Limit:* the filter stays when **perceiving is the event** ("He never saw the van") or when
the perception is doubtful and the doubt matters.

**M10b — The redundant pair.** Two words saying the same thing. The source's example is
"nodded his head"; others in the same vein: *whispered quietly, blinked her eyes, end
result, past history, completely finished*.

**M10c — The empty opening.** A sentence that clears its throat before speaking: *there was,
there were, it was … that, what happened was*. The real subject waits behind it.
- "There was a knock at the door." → "Someone knocked."
- "It was her brother who had sold the car." → "Her brother had sold the car."

*Limit:* *it was … who* stays when it **stresses a contrast** ("It was Tom who said it, not
me"). In dialogue, speech is entitled to its own turns.

**M10d — The softener.** *Just, really, very, a bit, quite, rather, almost, slightly*: they
dilute instead of pressing. "Very tired" is weaker than "exhausted"; "a bit worried" says
nothing "worried" does not.

*Limit:* in dialogue they are often the character's voice and stay. They also stay when the
nuance is the meaning ("almost dead" is not "dead").

*Examples for M10: apart from "nodded his head" and the crutch adverb "walked quickly" →
"hurried" (see M2), the source's examples are not reproduced; the M10 examples are original
to this file.*

**Why.** Each habit removes a little immediacy; together they make prose that is correct
and flat. None is a mistake, which is why they go unseen.

**What to check.** In the narration, not in dialogue: every perception verb in front of what
is perceived (M10a), every *there was* or *it was … that* at the head of a sentence (M10c),
every *very, really, just, a bit* (M10d). Redundant pairs (M10b) are spotted by reading.
Counting is pointless: flag the case where the habit **costs** something, that is, in a
scene that matters.
